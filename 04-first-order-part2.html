1 Recall · L and C at steady state

With all sources constant, at steady state all currents and voltages are constant. Then:

\( i = \text{const} \Rightarrow v_L = L\dfrac{di}{dt} = 0 \) inductor ⇒ short circuit
\( v = \text{const} \Rightarrow i_C = C\dfrac{dv}{dt} = 0 \) capacitor ⇒ open circuit
🗝️ This is the whole trick of the procedure

“Steady state” means replace L by a short and C by an open, then do plain DC analysis. Every initial value and every final value in this lecture comes from exactly that move.

2 Steady-state examples

Slides’ Examples 1–2, in the same form: “find the inductor currents and capacitor voltages at steady state.” At steady state, short the inductors and open the capacitors.

Example 1 (adapted) — 24 V, 9 Ω

24 V source feeding a 9 Ω series resistor into a node where L and C hang to ground in parallel.

The node is shorted to ground through L, so its DC voltage is 0. No current can enter the opened C branch.

\[ v_C(\infty) = 0\ \text{V}, \qquad i_L(\infty) = \frac{24\ \text{V}}{9\ \Omega} = 2.67\ \text{A} \]

Example 2 (adapted) — 24 V, 4 Ω / 8 Ω

24 V source through 4 Ω into node B; an 8 Ω to ground and an inductor to ground from B; a capacitor (in series with 3 Ω) tapping node B.

Node B is pulled to 0 V by the shorted inductor; the C branch (open) carries no current, so the 3 Ω drops nothing.

\[ i_L(\infty) = \frac{24\ \text{V}}{4\ \Omega} = 6\ \text{A}, \qquad v_C(\infty) = 0\ \text{V} \]

3 The general 4-step procedure

This is the single most useful procedure of the course. For any first-order circuit that changes at t = 0, and any response x(t):

Step 1  Find x(0⁺)

Reduce the t < 0 circuit to its DC steady state (L short, C open). Read the state variable, then use continuity (vC and iL don’t jump): x(0⁺) = x(0⁻).

Step 2  Find x(∞)

Reduce the t > 0 circuit to its DC steady state (L short, C open). Read the final value of the response.

Step 3  Find τ

Find the Thévenin resistance Rth seen by the single storage element in the t > 0 circuit, then \( \tau = R_{th}C \) or \( \tau = L/R_{th} \).

Step 4  Write the response

\[ x(t) = x(\infty) + \big[x(0^+) - x(\infty)\big]\,e^{-t/\tau} \]
🧮 Why one formula covers everything

The t > 0 solution is “forced + natural.” The forced part is x(∞) (the DC steady state of the final circuit), and the natural part is a single exponential whose amplitude is whatever makes the formula match x(0⁺) at t = 0. The 4 steps are just bookkeeping for that logic — no differential equations required.

4 Step 3 in detail · τ via the Thévenin resistance

For the t > 0 circuit, look into the terminals where the single storage element sits:

  1. Deactivate the independent sources — voltage sources become shorts, current sources become opens.
  2. Reduce the resistor network seen by the element to one resistance: Rth.
  3. Compute \( \tau = R_{th}C \) (or \( L/R_{th} \)).
⚠️ Two frequent slips

(a) The R in τ is not necessarily a single literal resistor — it’s the whole network as seen from L or C. (b) Dependent sources are not deactivated; with them in the circuit, find Rth = vtest/itest with a test source at the terminals.

Remove C, kill the source (12 V → short), reduce what’s left: 4 kΩ ∥ 2 kΩ.

5 Worked example · the procedure, end to end

Slide-style example: a capacitor that has sat on a 12 V supply is switched at t = 0 onto a 4 V supply through 4 kΩ, with C = 0.5 µF. Find vC(t) and i(t) for t ≥ 0.

C is open at steady state, so no current in the 4 kΩ and the full source appears on it:

\[ v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-) = 12\ \text{V} \]

Eventually the capacitor sits on the 4 V supply with no current again:

\[ v_C(\infty) = 4\ \text{V} \]

Deactivate the 4 V source (short). The only resistance left at C’s terminals is the 4 kΩ:

\[ R_{th} = 4\ \text{k}\Omega \quad\Rightarrow\quad \tau = R_{th}C = (4\times10^3)(0.5\times10^{-6}) = 2\ \text{ms} \]
\[ v_C(t) = 4 + (12 - 4)\,e^{-t/2\text{ms}} = 4 + 8\,e^{-t/2\text{ms}}\ \text{V} \]
\[ i(t) = C\,\frac{dv_C}{dt} = -16\,e^{-t/2\text{ms}}\ \text{mA} \]

The voltage falls from 12 V toward 4 V; the current is a negative exponential that jumps from 0 to −16 mA at t = 0⁺.

6 The unit step function

Switching a source in or out of a circuit is just a multiplication by a step. The unit step is defined by

(4.1) \[ u(t) = \begin{cases} 0, & t < 0 \\ 1, & t \ge 0 \end{cases} \] A voltage source that turns on at t = 0 is V·u(t); a current source that turns on is I·u(t).
Translated: \( u(t - t_0) \) step that turns on at t = t₀ (e.g. a pulse start)
Inverted: \( u(t_0 - t) \) on until t = t₀, then off (e.g. a pulse end)

7 Example 9 (slide-style) · sources written with u(t)

A 24 V source in series with 6 Ω, and a 2 A current source, both switched on at t = 0, feed an inductor L = 12 H to ground. Find iL(t) for t ≥ 0.

For t < 0 the sources are off (multiplied by u(t) = 0): the inductor carries nothing, and inductor current is continuous:

\[ i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-) = 0 \]

At t → ∞ the inductor is a short, so the node it sits on is at 0 V. By KCL at that node:

\[ i_L(\infty) = \frac{24}{6} + 2 = 6\ \text{A} \]

Seen from L: deactivate the 24 V (short) and the 2 A (open) — only the 6 Ω remains:

\[ \tau = \frac{L}{R_{th}} = \frac{12}{6} = 2\ \text{s} \]
\[ i_L(t) = 6 + (0 - 6)\,e^{-t/2} = 6\left(1 - e^{-t/2}\right)\ \text{A}, \quad t \ge 0 \]

8 Pulse excitation · two intervals

A rectangular pulse of amplitude Vp and width tp that starts at t = 0 is written as a difference of shifted steps:

(4.2) \[ v_{in}(t) = V_p\,\big[u(t) - u(t - t_p)\big] \] A pulse is “a step up, and later a step down.” First-order responses therefore come in two intervals.

Interval 1 · 0 < t < tₚ (pulse on)

The circuit sees a step of Vp. Apply the 4-step procedure with x(0⁺) and x(∞) = value if the pulse never ended:

\[ x(t) = x_1(\infty) + \big[x(0^+) - x_1(\infty)\big]e^{-(t-0)/\tau} \]

Interval 2 · t > tₚ (pulse off)

The new initial condition is the state left at t = tₚ (continuity!). The new final value is the DC steady state of the post-pulse circuit:

\[ x(t) = x_2(\infty) + \big[x(t_p) - x_2(\infty)\big]e^{-(t-t_p)/\tau} \]
🧠 The one rule that makes pulses easy

At every switching instant the state variable (vC or iL) carries over unchanged. Every interval is a fresh 4-step problem whose “initial value” is simply the previous interval’s ending value.

9 Summary

📌

• At DC steady state: L = short, C = open — that single rule gives x(0⁻) and x(∞).

• 4-step procedure: x(0⁺) → x(∞) → τ (via Rth seen by L or C) → \( x(t) = x(\infty) + [x(0^+) - x(\infty)]e^{-t/\tau} \).

• The unit step u(t − t₀) models switching; a pulse is a difference of two steps.

• Pulsed excitation = two intervals; each interval reuses the 4-step procedure with the previous interval’s ending state as its initial condition.

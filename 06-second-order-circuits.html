Second-Order Circuits
Natural Response

Two energy-storage elements change the story: the governing equation is now second-order, its roots come in pairs, and the transient can decay fast, decay just fast enough, or ring. This lecture covers the undriven series and parallel RLC — the characteristic equation, \(\alpha\), \(\omega_0\), and the three damping regimes — closed out by the deck's two worked examples.

🎯 Natural response of series & parallel RLC ✏️ Classify overdamped / critical / underdamped 📐 Six-step solution procedure

1 What is a second-order circuit?

A second-order circuit contains two energy-storage elements with independent states — for example a capacitor and an inductor whose initial voltage and initial current can be set separately. Its behavior is described by a second-order differential equation, so the natural response is built from two roots instead of one, and two initial conditions are needed to pin down the answer.

🕰️ Clock pendulums

Mass, spring and damper — the mechanical archetype every RLC lab re-enacts.

🚗 Automobile suspensions

Engineers tune \(\alpha\) so the body settles quickly without bouncing — critical damping in action.

📻 Tuned radio filters

LC “tanks” select a station; poorly damped inter-chip digital interconnections ring instead.

🎯 Student outcomes (slide)

• Determine the natural response of series and parallel RLC circuits.
• Classify the response of second-order circuits.

2 The undriven LC circuit

Undriven LC: no source, no resistance — internal (homogeneous) behavior only.

Before \(t = 0\) something energized the circuit; from then on it is undriven (no external stimuli) and lossless, so it is the cleanest place to see what a second-order system does on its own. The two elements share one voltage, and KCL at the top node says the currents cancel:

integrated form (KCL) \[ C\,\frac{dv(t)}{dt} + \frac{1}{L}\int_{-\infty}^{t} v(\lambda)\,d\lambda = 0 \]

Differentiate once with respect to \(t\) and divide by \(C\) — the same move that killed the integral in the first-order lecture:

(6.1) \[ \boxed{\;\frac{d^{2}v(t)}{dt^{2}} + \frac{1}{LC}\,v(t) = 0\;} \] A second-order ODE with constant coefficients. Now solve for v(t).
🔁 Recall before we go on

The complete solution of any differential equation is homogeneous + particular: \(x(t) = x_h(t) + x_p(t)\). With no source, \(x_p = 0\) and only the natural response survives — for a first-order circuit that was the exponential \(x_h = k\,e^{-t/\tau}\). Maybe a second-order circuit admits a similar guess…

Assume the exponential form \(v(t) = A e^{st}\). Substituting into (6.1):

\[ A s^{2} e^{st} + A\,\frac{1}{LC}\,e^{st} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A e^{st}\left(s^{2} + \frac{1}{LC}\right) = 0 \] Either A = 0 (which forces v ≡ 0 — impossible for an energized circuit) or the factor vanishes.
characteristic equation \[ s^{2} + \frac{1}{LC} = 0 \]
its roots \[ s_{1,2} = \pm\, j\,\omega_{0}, \qquad \omega_{0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \] Purely imaginary — ω₀ is the natural frequency (a.k.a. undamped natural frequency).

Superpose the two exponential solutions and apply Euler's identity \(e^{\pm j\theta} = \cos\theta \pm j\sin\theta\):

\[ v(t) = A_{1}e^{j\omega_{0}t} + A_{2}e^{-j\omega_{0}t} = (A_{1}+A_{2})\cos\omega_{0}t + j(A_{1}-A_{2})\sin\omega_{0}t = K_{1}\cos\omega_{0}t + K_{2}\sin\omega_{0}t \]

The two constants are fixed by the initial conditions. Since \(v(0) = v_C(0^+)\) and \(\dfrac{dv(0)}{dt} = \dfrac{i_C(0^+)}{C} = -\dfrac{i_L(0^+)}{C}\) (KCL), matching the equation to the circuit at \(t=0\) gives \(K_1 = v_C(0^+)\) and \(K_2 = -\dfrac{i_L(0^+)}{\omega_0 C} = -\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{C}}\,i_L(0^+)\):

(6.2) \[ \boxed{\;v(t) = v_C(0^+)\,\cos\omega_{0}t \;-\; \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}\; i_L(0^+)\,\sin\omega_{0}t\;} \] The whole undriven LC response from two initial conditions and one frequency.
👀 Remarks (slide)

• The capacitor voltage and the inductor current are both sinusoidal.
• The frequency is the magnitude of the natural frequency, \(\omega_0\).
• \(v_C\) leads \(i_L\) by 90° — and with no R to bleed energy, the oscillation never dies. Run Lab 3 and watch the energy juggle forever.

3 Why add a resistor at all?

Real tanks are never lossless. Resistance is naturally occurring — dielectric leakage in capacitors, winding resistance in inductors — and engineers add it on purpose when they want the oscillations to decay instead of ringing forever. The two typical models we will derive are the series and parallel RLC circuits:

Series RLC — \(\alpha = R/2L\): more R means more damping.
Parallel RLC — \(\alpha = 1/2RC\): more R means less damping.

4 Undriven series RLC — from KVL to the ODE

With the elements in one loop, all of them carry the same current \(i(t)\). KVL around the loop (the deck's derivation, slide by slide):

KVL, in element relations \[ v_C + v_R + v_L = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{C}\int_{-\infty}^{t} i\,d\lambda \;+\; R\,i(t) \;+\; L\,\frac{di(t)}{dt} = 0 \]

Differentiate both sides with respect to \(t\) and divide by \(L\):

(6.3) \[ \boxed{\;\frac{d^{2}i(t)}{dt^{2}} + \frac{R}{L}\,\frac{di(t)}{dt} + \frac{1}{LC}\,i(t) = 0\;} \] The ODE governing the loop current i(t).

Now the same exponential trick as the LC tank — assume \(i(t) = A e^{st}\) and substitute:

\[ A e^{st}\,s^{2} + \frac{R}{L}\,A s\, e^{st} + \frac{1}{LC}\,A e^{st} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A e^{st}\left(s^{2} + \frac{R}{L}\,s + \frac{1}{LC}\right) = 0 \] \[ \Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;s^{2} + \frac{R}{L}\,s + \frac{1}{LC} = 0\;} \] Characteristic equation of the undriven series RLC.

5 The characteristic equation and its roots

Quadratic formula with \(a = 1\), \(b = R/L\), \(c = 1/LC\):

\[ s_{1,2} = -\frac{R}{2L} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{R}{2L}\right)^{2} - \frac{1}{LC}} \]

The slide names the pieces that appear here — these three quantities decide everything that follows:

damping factor
\[ \alpha = \frac{R}{2L}\;\Big[\tfrac{\text{Np}}{\text{s}}\Big] \]

How fast the transients die out.

undamped natural frequency
\[ \omega_{0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}\;\Big[\tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}}\Big] \]

Also called the undamped resonant frequency.

natural frequencies
\[ s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^{2} - \omega_{0}^{2}} \]

The roots — measured in Np/s.

standard form \[ s^{2} + 2\alpha\, s + \omega_{0}^{2} = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^{2} - \omega_{0}^{2}} \]

So the natural response is always built from the two roots:

\[ i(t) = A_{1}e^{s_{1}t} + A_{2}e^{s_{2}t} = e^{-\alpha t}\left( A_{1}\,e^{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}}\,t} + A_{2}\,e^{-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}}\,t} \right) \] A₁ and A₂ are still unknown — the initial conditions fix them (procedure steps 5–6).

6 Three response types

The responses of \(v_C\) and \(i_L\) depend entirely on the relation between \(\alpha\) and \(\omega_0\) — that is, on the sign of \(\alpha^{2} - \omega_{0}^{2}\):

RegimeConditionRoots s₁,₂Form of the response
Overdamped \( \alpha > \omega_{0} \) two real, distinct \( x(t) = A_1 e^{s_1 t} + A_2 e^{s_2 t} \)
Critically damped \( \alpha = \omega_{0} \) two real, equal \( x(t) = \left(A_1\,t + A_2\right) e^{-\alpha t} \)
Underdamped \( \alpha < \omega_{0} \) complex conjugate: \( -\alpha \pm j\omega_d \) \( x(t) = e^{-\alpha t}\big( B_1 \cos\omega_d t + B_2 \sin\omega_d t \big) \)
🧭 How to read the table

Compare \(\alpha\) and \(\omega_0\) before you write anything down. The inequality picks the row; the row gives you the form; the initial conditions give you the coefficients. Lab 1 sweeps all three rows live — drag R and watch the regime flip.

7 Overdamped — \(\alpha > \omega_0\)

Here \(\alpha^{2} - \omega_{0}^{2} > 0\), so the two roots are real and distinct — two pure exponentials, no oscillation anywhere:

\[ \boxed{\;x(t) = A_{1}e^{s_{1}t} + A_{2}e^{s_{2}t}\;} \qquad s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^{2} - \omega_{0}^{2}} \]
🔗 The large-R limit (slide) — a bridge to Lectures 3–4

When \(R\) becomes very large, the roots become the reciprocals of first-order time constants:

\[ s_{1} = -\alpha\!\left(1 + \sqrt{1 - \tfrac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}}\right) \approx -2\alpha = -\frac{R}{L} = -\frac{1}{\tau_{L}}, \qquad s_{2} = -\alpha\!\left(1 - \sqrt{1 - \tfrac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}}\right) \approx -\alpha\,\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{2\alpha^{2}} = -\frac{1}{RC} = -\frac{1}{\tau_{C}} \]

Heavy damping quietly degenerates into the two first-order circuits you already know, acting one after the other — the inductor’s \(\tau_L = L/R\) pass, then the capacitor’s \(\tau_C = RC\) pass.

8 Critically damped — \(\alpha = \omega_0\)

Now the two roots are real and equal: \(e^{s_1 t}\) and \(e^{s_2 t}\) are the same function, no longer independent — so the second linearly-independent solution picks up a factor of \(t\):

\[ \boxed{\;x(t) = \left(A_{1}\,t + A_{2}\right) e^{s\,t}\;} \qquad \left(s_{1} = s_{2} = -\alpha = -\omega_0\right) \] Returns to zero in the shortest possible time without oscillating.
🚗 Why “critical” matters in practice

Underdamped is the fastest-looking but it overshoots and rings; overdamped is safe but sluggish. Critical damping is the fastest response that never crosses zero — which is exactly why suspension (and speaker, and op-amp) designers aim for it.

9 Underdamped — \(\alpha < \omega_0\)

Finally \(\alpha^{2} - \omega_{0}^{2} < 0\), so the square root is imaginary. Noting that \(j = \sqrt{-1}\) and defining the damped natural frequency:

\[ \omega_{d} = \sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2} - \alpha^{2}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm j\,\omega_{d} \]

Back through Euler — the LC-tank move, this time with the decay factor carried along for the ride:

\[ x(t) = e^{-\alpha t}\left( A_{1}e^{j\omega_d t} + A_{2}e^{-j\omega_d t} \right) = e^{-\alpha t}\left[ (A_{1}+A_{2})\cos\omega_d t + j(A_{1}-A_{2})\sin\omega_d t \right] \] (6.4) \[ \boxed{\;x(t) = e^{-\alpha t}\left( B_{1}\cos\omega_{d} t + B_{2}\sin\omega_{d} t \right)\;} \] A sinusoid at ω_d wrapped in the envelope ±e^{−αt}. Note ω_d < ω₀ — damping slows the ringing.

10 The undriven parallel RLC

Same game from the other side: all three elements share one node voltage \(v(t)\), and KCL at the top node gives the mirror image of the series derivation:

KCL, in element relations \[ i_R + i_L + i_C = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{v(t)}{R} + \frac{1}{L}\int_{-\infty}^{t} v(\lambda)\,d\lambda + C\,\frac{dv(t)}{dt} = 0 \]

Differentiate both sides, divide by \(C\), and run the \(A e^{st}\) substitution — the parallel ODE and characteristic equation fall out immediately:

parallel RLC ODE \[ \frac{d^{2}v}{dt^{2}} + \frac{1}{RC}\,\frac{dv}{dt} + \frac{1}{LC}\,v = 0 \]
standard form, again \[ s^{2} + 2\alpha s + \omega_{0}^{2} = 0 \] \[ \alpha = \frac{1}{2RC}, \qquad \omega_{0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^{2} - \omega_{0}^{2}} \]
⚠️ The one asymmetry to memorize

\(\omega_0\) is identical for series and parallel RLC — but \(\alpha\) flips its dependence on \(R\): \(\alpha_{series} = R/2L\) (more \(R\) → more damping) versus \(\alpha_{parallel} = 1/2RC\) (more \(R\) → less damping, because in parallel the resistor is a leak that removes energy from the node).

And the response categorizes into exactly the same three types — now for the node voltage \(v(t)\):

overdamped · α > ω₀
\[ v(t) = A_{1}e^{s_{1}t} + A_{2}e^{s_{2}t} \]
critical · α = ω₀
\[ v(t) = \left(A_{1}t + A_{2}\right)e^{-\alpha t} \]
underdamped · α < ω₀
\[ v(t) = e^{-\alpha t}\big(B_{1}\cos\omega_d t + B_{2}\sin\omega_d t\big) \]

with \(s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm j\omega_d\), \(\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^{2}-\alpha^{2}}\).

11 Ringing — underdamping in the real world

The oscillatory underdamped response is physically the two storage elements juggling energy back and forth. In real hardware this shows up as ringing — the wobble after the edge of a signal — exhibited by poorly designed amplifiers and by semiconductor devices carrying lots of parasitic inductances and capacitances.

🐛 Why designers hate it (slide)

Ringing increases the rise time or fall time of the output waveform — in digital terms it inflates what we call the propagation delay. Too little damping and every edge wobbles; too much and the signal is sluggish. Second-order design is the art of choosing \(\alpha\).

12 The six-step procedure

Every natural-response RLC problem in the deck — and on the exam — runs through this exact sequence. The two worked examples below are nothing but these six steps executed.

Before switching: the old steady state (capacitor → open, inductor → short). After switching: a new circuit to analyze. And recall the continuity properties: \(v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-)\) and \(i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)\) — energy can’t teleport.

Find the four quantities: \(v_C(0^+)\), \(\dfrac{dv_C(0^+)}{dt}\), \(i_L(0^+)\), \(\dfrac{di_L(0^+)}{dt}\). One comes straight from continuity; the derivative follows from a KVL/KCL snapshot at \(t = 0^+\) using \(\dfrac{dv_C}{dt} = \dfrac{i_C}{C}\) and \(\dfrac{di_L}{dt} = \dfrac{v_L}{L}\).

\[ \alpha_{series} = \frac{R}{2L}, \qquad \alpha_{parallel} = \frac{1}{2RC}, \qquad \omega_{0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \]

Compare \(\alpha\) and \(\omega_0\) and pick the form: overdamped \(A_1e^{s_1t} + A_2e^{s_2t}\) · critically damped \((A_1t + A_2)e^{-\alpha t}\) · underdamped \(e^{-\alpha t}(B_1\cos\omega_d t + B_2\sin\omega_d t)\).

Match the equation to the circuit at \(t = 0^+\): the value in the equation equals the value in the circuit, and the derivative in the equation equals the derivative in the circuit. Two equations, two unknowns.

Substitute all values into the determined response type, valid for \(t \ge 0\).

13 Example 1 — series RLC, underdamped

The capacitor is charged to 100 V and at \(t = 0\) the switch closes. Find \(i(t)\) for \(t \ge 0\), with \(R = 560\ \Omega\), \(L = 0.1\ \text{H}\), \(C = 0.1\ \mu\text{F}\).

Charged C, un-energized L — the switch hands the energy to the loop.

One loop after switching, so it is a series RLC. Note the initial inductor current is zero: at \(t = 0^+\) the whole capacitor voltage sits across the inductor, since \(v_R = Ri = 0\).

🧪 Follow along live

Lab 1 opens on exactly these values — \(R = 560\ \Omega\), \(L = 100\ \text{mH}\), \(C = 100\ \text{nF}\), \(v_C(0^+) = 100\ \text{V}\), \(i_L(0^+) = 0\). When you reach the answer, the plotted \(i(t)\) is this very curve.

\[ v_C(0^+) = 100\ \text{V}, \qquad i_L(0^+) = 0\ \text{A} \]
\[ \alpha = \frac{R}{2L} = \frac{560}{2(0.1)} = 2800\ \tfrac{\text{Np}}{\text{s}}, \qquad \omega_{0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.1 \times 0.1\mu}} = 10{,}000\ \tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}} \]

\(\alpha < \omega_0\) ⇒ underdamped.

\[ \omega_{d} = \sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2} - \alpha^{2}} = \sqrt{10{,}000^{2} - 2{,}800^{2}} = 9{,}600\ \tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}} \] \[ i(t) = e^{-2800t}\left( B_{1}\cos 9600t + B_{2}\sin 9600t \right) \]

\(i(0)\) in the equation must equal \(i_L(0^+)\) in the circuit:

\[ i(0) = B_{1} = i_L(0^+) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad B_{1} = 0 \]

Differentiate the equation and match it to \(\dfrac{di_L(0^+)}{dt} = \dfrac{v_L(0^+)}{L}\) in the circuit — and \(v_L(0^+) = v_C(0^+) - Ri(0^+) = 100\ \text{V}\):

\[ \frac{di(0)}{dt} = -\alpha B_{1} + \omega_{d} B_{2} = \frac{v_L(0^+)}{L} = \frac{100}{0.1} = 1000 \] \[ -2800(0) + 9600\,B_{2} = 1000 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad B_{2} = 0.1042 \]
\[ \boxed{\;i(t) = 0.1042\, e^{-2800 t}\, \sin(9600 t)\ \ \text{A}, \qquad t \ge 0\;} \]

A decaying sinusoid: the energy sloshes between C and L while R slowly bleeds it off — about four rings before the loop settles.

14 Example 2 — parallel RLC, overdamped

Given \(v(0^+) = V_0 = 12\ \text{V}\) and \(i_L(0^+) = I_0 = 30\ \text{mA}\), find \(v(t)\) for \(t \ge 0\), with \(R = 200\ \Omega\), \(L = 0.05\ \text{H}\), \(C = 0.2\ \mu\text{F}\).

\[ v_C(0^+) = 12\ \text{V}, \qquad i_L(0^+) = 30\ \text{mA} \]
\[ \alpha = \frac{1}{2RC} = \frac{1}{2(200)(0.2\mu)} = 12{,}500\ \tfrac{\text{Np}}{\text{s}}, \qquad \omega_{0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.05 \times 0.2\mu}} = 10{,}000\ \tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}} \]

\(\alpha > \omega_0\) ⇒ overdamped.

\[ s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^{2} - \omega_{0}^{2}} = -12{,}500 \pm \sqrt{12{,}500^{2} - 10{,}000^{2}} = -12{,}500 \pm 7{,}500 \] \[ s_{1} = -5{,}000\ \tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}}, \qquad s_{2} = -20{,}000\ \tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}} \] \[ v(t) = A_{1}e^{-5000t} + A_{2}e^{-20000t} \]

\(v(0)\) in the equation must equal \(v(0^+)\) in the circuit:

\[ v(0) = A_{1}e^{0} + A_{2}e^{0} = A_{1} + A_{2} = V_{0} = 12 \]

Differentiate the equation and match it to \(\dfrac{dv(0^+)}{dt} = \dfrac{i_C(0^+)}{C}\) in the circuit. KCL at \(t=0^+\) gives \(i_C(0^+) = -\big(i_L(0^+) + i_R(0^+)\big)\), with \(i_R(0^+) = v(0^+)/R\):

\[ -5000A_{1} - 20000A_{2} = -\frac{i_L(0^+) + i_R(0^+)}{C} = -\frac{0.03 + \frac{12}{200}}{0.2\mu} = -450{,}000 \]
\[ A_{1} + A_{2} = 12, \qquad -5000A_{1} - 20000A_{2} = -450{,}000 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A_{1} = -14, \quad A_{2} = 26 \] \[ \boxed{\;v(t) = -14\,e^{-5000 t} + 26\,e^{-20000 t}\ \ \text{V}, \qquad t \ge 0\;} \]

Check: \(v(0^+) = -14 + 26 = 12\ \text{V}\) ✓. The curve starts at 12 V and bleeds off as two overlapping exponentials — no oscillation, because the roots are real. Lab 2 opens on exactly these values.

15 Summary

📌

• A second-order circuit contains two independent energy-storage elements, and its natural response obeys a second-order differential equation.

• The roots of the characteristic equation (the natural frequencies) are set by the damping factor \(\alpha\) and the undamped natural frequency \(\omega_0\): \(s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2}\).

• Overdamped (\(\alpha > \omega_0\)): two real, distinct roots — the response decays without oscillating.

• Critically damped (\(\alpha = \omega_0\)): two real, equal roots — the response returns to zero in the shortest time without oscillating.

• Underdamped (\(\alpha < \omega_0\)): complex conjugate roots — the response oscillates at the damped natural frequency \(\omega_d\) while decaying (ringing).

• Solve by the six-step procedure: initial conditions → damping factor & natural frequency → response type → unknown coefficients → complete response.

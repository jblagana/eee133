Capacitors and Inductors

The two energy-storage elements of circuit theory. The capacitor hoards charge in an electric field; the inductor hoards current in a magnetic field. Together they explain every transient you will meet in Lectures 3–5.

🎯 Recall properties & V-I behavior ⚡ Compute v, i, power, energy 🔌 Simplify C and L networks

1 What is a capacitor?

A capacitor consists of two conducting plates separated by an insulator (a dielectric). It stores energy in its electric field. The two plates can hold equal and opposite charges, and the separation — even a vacuum gap — is what lets a voltage build up across them.

The capacitor. Current flows into one plate and out the other — but never across the gap.

Where you see them

  • Tuning circuits of radio receivers — selecting which frequency you hear.
  • Dynamic memory elements (DRAM) — a charged plate is a stored bit.
  • Timing, filtering, power conditioning — and, from the next lecture, transients.

2 Capacitance

Capacitance is the ratio of the charge on one plate to the voltage between the two plates, measured in farads (F). One farad is enormous — practical parts are in µF and nF.

(2.1) \[ C \;=\; \frac{q(t)}{v(t)} \qquad \big[\text{F}\big] = \frac{\text{coulomb}}{\text{volt}} \] Charge on one plate over plate-to-plate voltage.
📐 Capacitance depends on the physical dimensions! For the parallel-plate geometry, \( C = \varepsilon\,\dfrac{A}{d} \): larger plate area A and a thinner gap d mean more capacitance; the dielectric constant \( \varepsilon \) sets how strongly the gap stores field.

3 Current–voltage relation of the capacitor

Start from charge. Because \( q(t) = C\,v(t) \), the current (rate of change of charge) is the derivative of the voltage — this single fact drives everything that follows:

(2.2) \[ i(t) \;=\; \frac{dq}{dt} \;=\; C\,\frac{dv(t)}{dt} \] Capacitor V-I relation (passive sign convention). Current is proportional to how fast v changes.

Reading (2.2) in reverse, voltage is the integral of current:

(2.3) \[ v(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{C}\int_{t_0}^{t} i(\lambda)\,d\lambda \;+\; v(t_0) \] Voltage “remembers” its whole past — this is why v(0⁻) = v(0⁺) later.
🔑 The derivative is the whole story Steady voltage → zero current (acts like an open circuit). Abruptly changing voltage → enormous current. A capacitor opposes changes in voltage.

4 Power and energy of the capacitor

Instantaneous power absorbed by the element is \( p(t) = v(t)\,i(t) \). Substituting \( i = C\,dv/dt \) and integrating from 0 (un-charged) up to the present voltage \( v(t) \):

\[ w(t) = \int_{0}^{t} p(\lambda)\,d\lambda = \int_{0}^{v(t)} C v\,\frac{dv}{dt}\,dt = \boxed{\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,C\,v(t)^2\;} \] Energy stored in the electric field. Always ≥ 0 — the capacitor never burns energy; it only stores and returns it.

Notice what the square does: the sign of the voltage does not matter — a −5 V capacitor stores the same energy as a +5 V one.

5 Remarks on the properties of the capacitor

📡 DC voltage ⇒ zero current

When the voltage across a capacitor is not changing with time, the current through it is zero. Under steady-state DC, a capacitor behaves as an open circuit.

🧵 Voltage must be continuous

An instantaneous change in v would demand \( i = C\,dv/dt \to \infty \), which an ideal source-free network cannot supply. Hence \( v(0^-) = v(0^+) \) across a capacitor.

♻️ Ideal ⇒ lossless

The ideal capacitor does not dissipate energy; it only shuttles it between field and source. Real (non-ideal) capacitors have a parallel leakage resistance that slowly bleeds the stored charge.

🚪 Un-charged ⇒ short circuit

A capacitor with zero initial voltage has v = 0 whether or not current flows — it behaves like a short circuit at the instant it starts charging. (See Lecture 3 for the transient that follows.)

7 Capacitors in DC circuits (worked example)

Slide example: “obtain the energy stored in each capacitor under DC conditions.” The recipe: replace every capacitor by an open circuit, solve the resistor-only circuit, then apply \( w = \tfrac12 C v^2 \).

With no DC current able to flow into a capacitor branch, the capacitors vanish (open), and the remaining resistors fix the node voltages. For the slide’s network (12 V source, 2 kΩ–2 kΩ divider feeding the capacitor nodes) the open-circuit solution gives v₁ = 8 V across C₁ and v₂ = 4 V across C₂.

\[ w_1 = \tfrac{1}{2} C_1 v_1^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.5\,\mu\text{F})(8\,\text{V})^2 = 16\ \mu\text{J} \]
\[ w_2 = \tfrac{1}{2} C_2 v_2^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.25\,\mu\text{F})(4\,\text{V})^2 = 2\ \mu\text{J} \]

DC ⇒ capacitors are open ⇒ no current into their branches ⇒ their terminal voltages equal the DC node voltages of the resistor network. If instead the question had been about the behavior right after a switch, an un-energized capacitor would model as a short at t = 0⁺ — that distinction is the heart of Lecture 3.

8 Capacitors in series and parallel

Capacitors combine the opposite way resistors do:

\[ C_{\text{eq}} = \sum_k C_k \quad (\text{parallel}) \]
\[ \frac{1}{C_{\text{eq}}} = \sum_k \frac{1}{C_k} \quad (\text{series}) \]
🤔 Why the opposite of resistors?

Parallel capacitors share the same voltage, so charges add: \( q_{eq} = \sum C_k v \Rightarrow C_{eq} = \sum C_k \). Series capacitors share the same charge, so voltages add: \( v_{eq} = \sum q/C_k \Rightarrow 1/C_{eq} = \sum 1/C_k \). Inductors (next section) behave like resistors — the exact mirror image of capacitors.

⚠️ Watch the initial conditions

Series capacitors each keep their own initial voltage; the equivalent capacitor’s initial voltage is found by superposing \( v_k = \sum q/C_k \). Don’t assume the series string starts “discharged” unless every member is.

9 What is an inductor?

An inductor is a conducting wire wound into a coil. It stores energy in the magnetic field surrounding it — a field that exists only while current is flowing. Where the capacitor said “voltage changes slowly,” the inductor says “current changes slowly.”

The inductor symbol: a chain of half-circles. The field wraps around every turn.

10 Inductance

Inductance is the ratio of the total magnetic flux linkage generated by a given current through the coil, measured in henrys (H). The coil’s physical dimensions — turns, area, length, core — set the value.

(2.4) \[ L \;=\; \frac{\lambda(t)}{i(t)} = \frac{N\,\Phi(t)}{i(t)} \qquad \big[\text{H}\big] = \frac{\text{weber·turn}}{\text{ampere}} \] Flux linkage λ = N·Φ over current. 1 H ⇒ 1 V appears across the coil when current ramps at 1 A/s.

11 Voltage–current relation of the inductor

Faraday: the voltage across a coil equals the rate at which its flux linkage changes. With \( \lambda = L\,i \), and signed for the passive sign convention:

(2.5) \[ v(t) \;=\; L\,\frac{di(t)}{dt} \] Inductor V-I relation. Voltage is proportional to how fast i changes.

And in reverse — current is the integral of voltage:

(2.6) \[ i(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{L}\int_{t_0}^{t} v(\lambda)\,d\lambda \;+\; i(t_0) \] The inductor “remembers” the whole past of its voltage — why i(0⁻) = i(0⁺).
🔑 The derivative is the whole story Steady current → zero voltage (acts like a short circuit). Abruptly changing current → enormous voltage. An inductor opposes changes in current — spark plugs and relay snubbers exist because of this very equation.

12 Power and energy of the inductor

Power absorbed is \( p(t) = v(t)\,i(t) \); with \( v = L\,di/dt \), integrate from zero current:

\[ w(t) = \int_{0}^{t} p(\lambda)\,d\lambda = \int_{0}^{i(t)} L i\,\frac{di}{dt}\,dt = \boxed{\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,L\,i(t)^2\;} \] Energy stored in the magnetic field — same square, same sign-insensitivity as the capacitor.

13 Remarks on the properties of the inductor

🔌 Zero voltage ⇒ constant current

When the voltage across an inductor is zero, the current through it is constant. Under steady-state DC, an inductor behaves as a short circuit.

🧵 Current must be continuous

Jumping current would demand \( v = L\,di/dt \to \infty \). Hence \( i(0^-) = i(0^+) \) through an inductor. This continuity is the key to every switched circuit in Lecture 3.

♻️ Ideal ⇒ lossless

The ideal inductor does not dissipate energy. Real (non-ideal) inductors have a series winding resistance that eventually bleeds the stored energy as heat.

🚪 Zero current ⇒ open circuit

An inductor carrying no current can have any voltage across it — like an open circuit — until something starts charging its field.

15 Inductors in series and parallel

Inductors combine like resistors — the exact opposite of capacitors:

\[ L_{\text{eq}} = \sum_k L_k \quad (\text{series}) \]
\[ \frac{1}{L_{\text{eq}}} = \sum_k \frac{1}{L_k} \quad (\text{parallel}) \]
🤔 Why like resistors?

Series inductors carry the same current, so voltages add: \( v_{eq} = \sum L_k\,di/dt \Rightarrow L_{eq} = \sum L_k \). Parallel inductors share the same voltage, so currents add. (If parallel inductors start with different currents, a circulating current can exist in the equivalent — keep that in mind for energy bookkeeping.)

17 Summary

📌

• Capacitors resist changes in voltage and store energy in their electric field.

• Inductors resist changes in current and store energy in their magnetic field.

• Under steady-state DC, a capacitor is an open circuit and an inductor a short circuit.

• An un-energized capacitor is a short; an un-energized inductor is an open — the two “initial” cases that open every problem in Lecture 3.
